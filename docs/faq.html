FAQ

What it is, what it needs, how it sounds, what breaks and why, and what happens to the sounds you make.

What it is

What is Abliterator?

It is an instrument for vector bending. It plays a generative music model live, and while the model plays it lets you change the vectors that it computes with. You choose a style prompt and a seed and press play, and then you shape the sound with the Steer pad, with units between the model's layers, with effects on its output, and with modulation. Sounds you like are saved with a recipe that replays them exactly, and can then be layered on eight tracks in Session.

Are the sounds on this site live?

No. They are recordings, made with the instrument on an Apple M5 and saved as MP3s: saved sounds, and renders from the measurement sweeps. A web page cannot run the model; it plays live only in the instrument, on your own machine. The spectrograms were computed from those recordings when the site was built; the scope and spectrum over the Listen cards are your browser's own analysis of what is playing.

Is it an AI music generator?

The model writes all of the audio, so in that sense yes. It is played the way a synthesiser is played: continuously, with knobs, and with what you hear about a tenth of a second after each move. There is no request and wait for a finished piece.

What does “Abliterator” mean?

The name comes from abliteration, removing a direction from a model's internal vectors, a technique first used to stop chat models refusing; the ending makes it an instrument, as oscillator, modulator and vocoder are. Abliterator uses the word for any change to a model's internal vectors while it runs, on a model that writes sound. What is Abliteration? has the history, and demonstrations of what it does to sound.

Who made it?

David M. Berry, at the University of Sussex, as a Vector Lab instrument. It is free software, GPL-3.0-or-later.

Sound

Do the Model layers process audio?

No. They act on the numbers the model computes with (its residual stream, 1,024 numbers per 40 ms frame), between and inside its transformer layers, before any audio exists. The effects are the part that processes audio, after the model. Model layers shows where each unit acts.

Can it play at a different tempo?

Not by asking. Magenta RT 2 has no tempo control, and if you write a tempo into the prompt it is ignored. In 30 renders of three beat presets, asked for tempos from 70 to 150 BPM, only 4 came out near the tempo asked for (about what you would get by chance), and Deep techno stayed at around 123 BPM whatever it was asked. What Abliterator can do is play the model's sound slower. The Speed control in the Engine panel goes down to an eighth. Tape lets the pitch fall, as a slowed tape would; Stretch keeps the pitch; and Smear keeps it but washes the sound out, for slow ambient. The model then writes only as fast as its sound is used.

Why is there a small v in the units' help (vLookback, vNorm Shrink)?

The v is VAU's mark for the units of its Core Collection, the vector effects the vau package ships. VAU names them for what they do to the stream: Lookback, Running Average, Norm Shrink and so on. Until VAU 0.2.3 they had studio names (vDelay, vReverb, vCompressor), which on an instrument with a real delay and reverb on its output would be taken for audio effects. A unit's card shows the name without the v. Frame Repeat is the one unit named differently here: VAU calls it Position Repeat, since its positions are tokens in a text model and frames of sound in this one.

Can I play my own audio into it?

Not in this version. The model plays from a style prompt and a seed.

Can I write my own prompts?

Yes. Type a style into the Prompt box, which is under the preset menu on the Engine panel, and press Enter. The engine makes the prompt's tokens (this takes about 90 ms) and plays it, or switches to it if the model is already playing. You will then find the prompt under Your prompts in the menu, and in data/prompts.json, and you can remove it with ✕. The twenty-five presets are themselves prompts, with sampler settings and seeds, and they are kept in backend/config/presets.md as a block you can edit. If you edit it, reload the page. The knobs' ranges were measured on the ambient presets, so a new style uses those ranges until it has been swept in Tune.

How do I modulate a knob?

Drag a source's tile (an LFO, an envelope, a random source, or a macro from the Macros tab) onto the knob. A badge with the source's shape follows the pointer as you drag, and the knobs that can take it light up. With the source selected, each knob that it can move shows its send. Drag the knob up for more and down for less, and double-click it to unlink. Pressing Esc turns the knobs back into their values. You can modulate the Steer doses, Strength, Temperature, the low-pass and the volume, as well as every knob of a Model layers unit and of an effect. An LFO's Amount scales every send from it at once.

Why do some settings break the sound, and what are the ⚠ marks?

A model's controls do not stay musical over the whole of their range. A Steer dose of 0.4, for example, overdrives the Warm drone, and Lookback at a mix of 0.5 silences it. This is why every control and unit setting was swept on several presets and measured, so that each knob now keeps to the range in which the sound held. If you see a ⚠ on a unit's knob or placement, that setting broke the sound on at least one preset. The ranges were measured with each unit's old defaults, and with Keep size off. This means that where a card now starts outside them (as Lookback and Running Average do, with Keep size on), its knobs turn over the whole of their range and you will not see the marks. You can unlock the doses with Full range on the Steer card. In the Tune view (◎) you can listen to every setting that was measured, and set a knob's range by ear.

Where do the presets on a card come from?

Each unit's ▾ menu lists five to seven presets. These were chosen from 163 settings measured on six seeds of the Warm drone, and then checked on ten other seeds. A preset was only kept if it broke the sound on none of those ten. Its tip names what moved on those seeds beyond chance, that is, brighter or darker, louder or quieter, the shape of the spectrum, or how alike neighbouring frames are. Each effect also has five to seven presets, measured on drones, a harp, a modular synth and a drum loop. In the same menu, under User Presets, Save preset… keeps a card's settings under a name, and the preset then appears on every card of that unit or effect; × deletes one. Tech specs has the figures for every preset.

Why does it sometimes drop out?

The model writes each 40 ms frame in about 25 ms on an Apple M5, and at the Buffer's default the engine keeps two frames (80 ms) ahead of the sound card. A dropout is a step that runs past what the frames in hand can cover, which is about 55 ms. On this instrument, the steps that do this are almost all caused by another program loading the computer (a browser starting up, a screenshot, another model on the GPU). These stretch a few steps out to 50 to 120 ms, while the rest stay at 25. If a step runs long, or the card runs dry, the engine now holds one to four more frames by itself, and gives them back one at a time once there has been a quiet minute (the Buffer knob shows them, as “80 +40 ms”). GPU load in the top bar shows whichever is slower, the steps' 95th percentile over ten seconds or the slowest step in the last two, so that a spike will show there. You can also give the model time in hand by running at a Speed below 1×, because it writes only as fast as its sound is used. With Smear or Stretch the sound card's callback does more work. Indeed, while a model step was running it took up to 16 ms of its 10.7 ms block. For this reason the card holds 40 ms of sound, so that a late callback is covered, since macOS plays silence for the shortfall and does not report an underflow. When a dropout does happen, the last block plays on, mirrored and fading over 30 ms, and the sound then crossfades back in over 5 ms, so you will not hear a click. Saved sounds are taken from the frames the model wrote, which means a dropout you heard live will not be in them.

Why do I hear clicks with some units?

A unit that pushes the model hard can make it write short bursts, where a few samples swing up and down, and these sound like two pieces of audio that have been badly spliced. Neuron at a large scale and Quantise at 6 bits or fewer are the units that do this most. The clicks are in the model's output and do not come from the playback. The engine logs the sound card's underflows, and any jump between the blocks it sends, and neither of these appears when the clicks happen. De-click in Output (on by default) redraws each burst as a straight line between the samples on either side, with 1.3 ms of look-ahead. If you turn it off, you hear what the model wrote.

Why does a unit's sound stay after I switch it off?

The model writes each frame by reading back the frames it has just written. Each of its twelve layers looks at the last 41 frames (1.6 s), and together they reach back about 20 s. Frames made while a unit was on are therefore still in what the model reads after the unit goes off, so it goes on making that sound for a while, and it may settle somewhere new instead of returning to where it was. Snap-back (an on/off item on each unit's ▾ menu, on by default) keeps the model's state from the moment you switch the unit on. When you switch the unit off or remove it, Snap-back returns the model to that state, crossfaded over one 40 ms frame, and from that frame on the model goes on exactly as it would have if the unit had never played. Without Snap-back, the Warm drone was still brighter ten seconds after Abliterate went off (a spectral centroid of 229 Hz, against 138 before). Deep techno, which its prompt holds more firmly, was back within two seconds either way. To let a unit's effect fade instead, turn Snap-back off on its ▾ menu. Saved sounds replay a snap-back as it was heard.

How long from a move to the sound?

With the default buffer of two frames it takes about 130 ms. Your move goes into the next frame that the model writes, and you hear it once that frame has reached the sound card, which holds about 49 ms.

What is the scope?

A meter you can put on the Model layers row. It draws the model's stream at its layer as the model plays: its shape, its bands, and how it moves, including its reading along the Brightness and Pulse directions. It changes nothing and is not saved with sounds.

Sounds and files

Can I get the same sound again?

Yes. The same seed, preset, settings and moves give the same audio, sample for sample. Every saved sound keeps its recipe, and the engine renders it again from the recipe, sample for sample.

What format are sounds saved in, and where?

WAV, 48 kHz stereo, 32-bit float, in data/snapshots/ (in the app, under ~/Library/Application Support/Abliterator/data/): the sound as you heard it (after the effects and the output section) and the model's own output. Session exports a track or the mix as WAV to data/exports/.

Can I use it with my DAW? Is it a plug-in?

Export WAV and bring it in. A plug-in for Ableton Live is planned, with the Synth panel as its interface, and not built. Abliterator is not affiliated with Ableton.

Models and machines

Which models does it play?

Magenta RealTime 2 small by default, in 8-bit on Apple's MLX. MusicGen small is a second engine, with Steer but without the model-layer units, and slower to answer (about 285 ms); it is chosen in Settings (⚙), Model. Both run through the same engine interface; adding another model means writing an engine for it.

What do I need?

A Mac with Apple silicon and macOS 14 or later. The app (a disk image on the latest release) installs everything else on its first run; from the source you need Python 3.12 and uv or pip. Magenta RT 2's weights take about 2.9 GB and are kept in $MAGENTA_HOME, by default ~/Documents/Magenta. It has been built and measured on an Apple M5 with 24 GB; other machines have not been tested.

Does it need the internet?

To install it, and to fetch the models. After that it runs on your own machine.

What licences apply?

Read them before you publish what you make. Abliterator is not affiliated with Google DeepMind or Meta.

The units

What is VAU?

Vector Abliteration Units, a standard and a Python package of small units that change, or read, what a model computes from inside. Abliterator installs the package and hosts its units on the Model layers row. Their PyTorch code is in the VAU package; this repository holds the host and MLX versions of Neuron, Quantise and Abliterate.

Can I write my own unit?

A unit is one Python file with a manifest, written to the VAU standard; the VAU repository describes how. Abliterator offers the units the installed VAU package provides.
